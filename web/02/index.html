How AI looks things up

Find the right paragraph first, then answer

Imagine a friend asks you a question about a storybook. You don't know the answer by heart, so you flip through the book, find the paragraph that talks about it, and read that part out loud.

AI tools do the same when they answer from your documents. First they look up the paragraphs that best match your question, then they answer using what they found. This is called RAG — "Retrieval-Augmented Generation", a fancy name for "look it up, then answer".

This page shows the looking-up part. It searches 4 short stories, split into paragraphs:

  • The Ant and the Grasshopper
  • The Boy Who Cried Wolf
  • The Three Little Pigs
  • The Tortoise and the Hare

It scores each paragraph by how many of your question's words it shares — rare words count more than common ones — and picks the best one.

6 examples: which paragraph did it find?

Each example shows a question, and the paragraph the search picked as the best match.

  1. You ask: Why was the grasshopper hungry in winter?

    Found in: The Ant and the Grasshopper

    When winter finally arrived, the ground froze and snow covered the fields. The grasshopper had no food stored away and grew hungry and cold, with no music left to play.

    ✅ Right paragraph. "Grasshopper", "hungry" and "winter" all appear in it.

  2. You ask: Why did the villagers stop believing the boy?

    Found in: The Boy Who Cried Wolf

    No one came to help, and the wolf scattered the flock. The shepherd boy learned a hard lesson that evening: once you have lied, people stop believing you even when you are telling the truth.

    ✅ Right paragraph. "Stop believing" is in the story, word for word.

  3. You ask: Why did the wolf blow down the straw house?

    Found in: The Three Little Pigs

    The wolf huffed and puffed with all his might, but the brick house did not budge. No matter how hard he tried, the wolf could not blow the sturdy brick house down, and the three pigs stayed safe inside.

    ❌ Right story, wrong part. You asked about the straw house, but it picked the brick house — because "wolf", "blow" and "house" show up there even more. It matches words, not meaning.

  4. You ask: What is the moral about lying?

    Found in: The Tortoise and the Hare

    The moral of the story is simple: slow and steady wins the race.

    ❌ Wrong story. The word "moral" matched the tortoise story. The story about lying says "lied", and to this search "lying" and "lied" are different words.

  5. You ask: What happens when you are dishonest?

    🤷 Nothing found — no story shares a word with this question.

    ❌ Missed. The Boy Who Cried Wolf is all about being dishonest — but it never uses the words in this question, so the search finds nothing.

  6. You ask: tax rules for crypto

    🤷 Nothing found — no story shares a word with this question.

    ✅ Honest. None of the stories are about taxes, so it says so instead of making up an answer.

The lesson: this search matches words, not meaning. Ask with the story's own words and it finds the right paragraph. Ask the same thing in other words and it misses, or picks the wrong one. Many AI tools today search by meaning instead — but the idea is the same: look it up first, then answer.

Try your own question

Ask anything about the 4 stories and see which paragraphs match best — and how well.

Tip: the search only matches whole words — the same words the story uses (capital letters don't matter). "grasshopper" finds the story, but "grass" finds nothing — to this search, they are different words.

This part needs a one-time download of up to 35 MB: Python itself, plus the search tool the demo uses. On mobile data, you may want to wait for Wi-Fi. After that, your browser keeps it and it's quick.

Everything on this page runs inside your own browser. Nothing is sent anywhere, and nothing is saved. This page never talks to an AI — it only does the looking-up part.
