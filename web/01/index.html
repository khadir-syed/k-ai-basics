How AI reads words

Tiny pieces first, then a guess

Imagine a big box of Lego bricks. You can't build anything with one giant brick — you need lots of small ones that snap together.

AI is the same with words. It can't read a sentence the way we do. First it cuts the sentence into small pieces called tokens — a whole word, part of a word, or even a single letter. Each token has an ID number, and the AI only ever sees those numbers.

Then it does the one thing it's really built for: it guesses which token comes next. Everything an AI writes, it writes one guessed token at a time.

What's the number under each token? It's the token's ID. GPT-2 has a numbered list of 50,257 tokens, and "Once" is number 7454 on it. The number isn't worked out from the letters — it's simply where "Once" sits in the list. GPT-2 made its list by reading lots of text. It started with 256 basic building blocks (numbers 0 to 255: single letters, digits, symbols, and bits of other letters — a comma is number 11). Then, again and again, it glued together the two pieces it saw side by side most often, and gave each new piece the next number. "·the" was glued very early, so it's number 262. "Once" was made from "O" + "nce", and got 7454. So a small number usually means a very common piece. The AI never sees the letters O-n-c-e — only the number 7454.

This page uses GPT-2, a small AI that OpenAI released in 2019 — old and simple, which makes it perfect for seeing the pieces.

The lesson: AI doesn't see words, it sees numbered pieces — and it doesn't know facts, it guesses the most likely next piece. Today's AIs are thousands of times bigger than GPT-2, so their guesses are far better. But under the hood, they still read tokens and guess the next one.
